The Tally

How many people were waiting for treatment in the NSW emergency departments that publish a live count — the last day of it, one stroke for each person, and a hand you can drag back through the night.

A count, not a wait. The feed publishes how many people are recorded as waiting for treatment; it has no minutes field, so nothing here is a waiting time. And 59 departments, not the state: NSW has 176 emergency departments, and these are the ones that publish a live count.

Reading it

One stroke is one person

One stroke is one person recorded as waiting for treatment at that department, at that quarter-hour. Every fifth stroke crosses the four before it, so a gate is five. The faint strokes behind are the department’s busiest reading of the day; a tile that is mostly faint is a department well below its own peak.

Drag the hand, or put the rail in focus and use the keys: ← → move a quarter-hour, with Shift an hour; Home and End go to the ends; Space plays the day through. The address follows the hand, so a link to a moment stays a link to that moment for as long as its day is the day published.

Provenance

Where the strokes come from

A collector on an always-on host takes a reading every fifteen minutes and appends it to a time series that is never rewritten. Once a day, around nine in the morning, it publishes the last twenty-four hours as a file the Queue Board also reads; this page reads that file when it opens, and the strip above names the day it holds. The collection’s own history, gaps included, is in the record; the code is in the public mirror.

Limits

What it is not

Not a clinical instrument: nothing here should inform a decision about where to seek care. Not a waiting time, because the feed has none. Not the whole state, because most emergency departments do not publish a live count. And not live to the minute: the file is published once a day, so the right-hand end of the rail is the last reading published, not the reading being taken now.
