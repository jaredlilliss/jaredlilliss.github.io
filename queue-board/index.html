Two things this is not. It is not a waiting time — the feed publishes a count of people waiting for treatment, and there is no minutes field anywhere in it. And it is not statewide — these are the emergency departments that publish live counts. NSW has 176.

Every reporting hospital

The vs its usual column compares each hospital's latest reading to its own average for that hour of the day — because a raw count means nothing across sites of different sizes. Twelve people waiting is quiet at one hospital and busy at another. Comparing against the hour matters: the state's own curve runs from 1.84 people waiting at 6am to 6.59 at 8pm, so measuring against an all-day average would mostly report what time you loaded the page. Hospitals averaging under two people waiting show an absolute difference instead of a percentage — at that size a ratio just reports whether one extra person arrived. There is no clinical severity scale here, and inventing one would be a claim this data cannot support. The trace shows that hospital's last 24 hours.

What the collection actually looked like

Every bar is one day, and its height is how many of the 96 possible readings were captured. This is the honest version: for its first month the collector ran on a Windows laptop, which suspends background processes when it idles, so most days are partial and five have nothing at all. On 23 August it moved to an always-on host. Nothing after that is missing.

You can tell which machine took a reading by looking at the clock

The always-on host runs on a fifteen-minute cron schedule, so its readings land at exactly :00, :15, :30 and :45 — 24 of each, 96 a day, never drifting. The laptop ran a sleeping loop, so its readings landed wherever it happened to wake: :02, :11, :26, :39.

The cutover day has 115 readings rather than 96 — the 96 clean quarter-hour ones from the new host, plus 19 drifting ones from the laptop still running alongside it. Both collectors were live for a few hours. No reading was ever double-counted: across all 77,000 rows there is not one duplicate of the same hospital at the same instant.

When your emergency department is unusually busy, is everyone else's?

Mostly not. That is the short answer, and it is not the one most people expect.

How this is measured, because the naive version would be misleading. Any two hospitals will appear to move together simply because both are quiet at dawn and busy in the evening — correlating the raw counts would mostly rediscover the daily cycle and dress it up as a finding. So each reading first has that hospital's own average for that hour of the day subtracted. What is left is the part that is unusual for that hospital, at that time.

Those leftovers are then compared against the same leftovers for the rest of the state, excluding the hospital being measured — otherwise a large hospital partly correlates with itself and scores too high. Every one of the 59 hospitals reported at all — shared moments, so no pair is being compared across a gap.

What that actually means

The most system-coupled hospital in the state shares about a sixth of its variation with everywhere else. The rest is its own. For the quieter hospitals it is close to nothing at all. So a surge at your local emergency department is overwhelmingly a local event — not a wave passing across New South Wales.

The one visible pattern is size, and part of it is arithmetic rather than medicine. Busier hospitals score roughly 0.22 against roughly 0.14 for quieter ones. Some of that may be real. But a small department's count is dominated by whether one or two people happened to arrive, and that randomness dilutes any shared signal — so a low score at a small hospital is partly a measurement effect, and should not be read as that community behaving differently.

The shaded band on the chart is noise. Readings fifteen minutes apart are not independent of each other — consecutive ones correlate at about 0.8 — so the usual formula would understate this band badly. Widened accordingly: anything inside roughly ±0.12 cannot be told apart from zero. Values below the band are not evidence that a hospital moves opposite to the state; they are consistent with no relationship at all.

Where this comes from. A privacy-by-design pipeline: no tables, columns or fields for people, accounts, sessions or devices exist anywhere in the schema — the privacy posture is structural, not a policy. Readings are collected every fifteen minutes on an always-on host and appended to a PostgreSQL time series that is never rewritten. The upstream feed serves current state only, with no historical endpoint, so a missed reading can never be backfilled — which is why the collector was built before any interface. © State of New South Wales NSW Ministry of Health.
